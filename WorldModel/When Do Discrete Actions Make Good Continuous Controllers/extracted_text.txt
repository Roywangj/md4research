Abstract
Though modern AI models like LLMs predict discrete tokens, today’s robotic foundation models
represent actions as continuous vectors. In this paper, we challenge this common consensus by asking
if continuous action parametrizations are superior to their discrete counterparts. We conduct an
extensive comparison of single-step regression and multi-step flow variants of both continuous and
discrete policies in both from-scratch single-task and multi-task VLA training, in both simulation
and on real hardware. In all regimes, we find that discrete action prediction, even the most naive,
uniform-bin quantization, can match or even outperform continuous-action policies in task success

Introduction
A major goal of modern robot learning is to perform complex and varied tasks to high precision. As a result,
the community has devoted significant study to the question of how to parametrize the actions predicted
by robotic policies and foundation models, such as vision-language-action models (VLAs) [Brohan et al.,
2023, Kim et al., 2024, Black et al., 2025, NVIDIA et al., 2025] and world action models (WAMs) [Li et al.,
2025, Ye et al., 2026b, Kim et al., 2026, Li et al., 2026b]. The common consensus is that robot models
should predict continuous-valued actions during deployment, as these are lossless and therefore support
high-precision behavior. For example, most of today’s VLAs predict actions using continuous-space
generative models, such as flow [Black et al., 2025, Physical Intelligence et al., 2025b] or diffusion
models [Chi et al., 2023], which are believed to better capture training data diversity whilst enhancing
the precision of action predictions.
At the same time, modern robot learning is adopting increasingly more features from other AI
domains, notably language modeling. These include test-time reasoning [Wei et al., 2022], on-task
self-correction [Huang et al., 2022], future goal prediction [Du et al., 2023], and long-term memory
[Wang et al., 2023]. In fact, recent work has showcased the possibility that frontier language models can
directly control robot embodiments through text generation [Brohan et al., 2023, Kim et al., 2024]. Yet,
today’s frontier AI models, even ones that process multiple modalities such as text, images, and audio,
both take as input and produce discretely quantized tokens. Whether robot actions should follow the
same discrete format therefore raises a key question:
Are robot actions unique among other AI modalities in that they must be predicted as continuous
values, or can discrete action representations compete with, or even outperform, their continuous
counterparts?
1.1
Contributions
In this work, we undertake a systematic comparison between the performance of continuous and discrete
action parameterizations for both single-task robot policies and multi-task vision-language-action (VLA)
models. In short, we show that discrete action parametrization can be competitive with, and even
uniformly outperform, continuous action prediction, provided that correct design decisions are made.
These findings provide a deeper understanding of the performance of recent discrete diffusion/flow VLA
policies [Liang et al., 2026b, Wen et al., 2025b, Chen et al., 2026a].
In greater detail, we consider parametrizations of the action head, or part of a robot model re-
sponsible for action prediction. Our comparison builds on classical supervised control and imitation
learning [Pomerleau, 1988, Ross et al., 2011, Osa et al., 2018]. We focus our study on 4 representative
parametrizations: (1) continuous regression (CR), which predicts continuous-valued actions in a single
forward pass and is trained with a regression objective [Bojarski et al., 2016, Kim et al., 2025] (2)

robot control requires predicting continuous action prediction:
• Finding 1: Control Precision Contrary to prior belief, discrete action parametrizations, even discrete
regression (DR) with the simplest bin discretization, can exhibit greater control precision than their
continuous counterparts, despite the possibly lossy quantization of actions! We support this claim in
both sim and real settings in Section 3. We show that this arises from greater policy reactivity, both to
the action-chunk time index and the conditioning observation.
• Finding 2: Iterative Refinement and Guidance Mitigate Factorization Error (Section 4). Inde-
pendently predicted tokens can combine incompatible choices across time and action dimensions,
even when individual token distributions are accurate. DF solves this by iterative computation, while
classifier-free guidance reduces ambiguity by changing token preferences. Both improve control signifi-
cantly, supporting joint consistency as main explanation for their gains. In the RoboCasa comparison,
guidance raises DR success from 27.1% to 49.2%, approaching DF’s 53.9% and CF’s 52.7%, largely
ahead of CR’s 38.8%.
• Finding 3: Explicit Geometry and Structured Tokenization Provide Limited Additional Gains
(Section 5). Encoding action distances or using alternative tokenizers improves some configurations,
but none exceeds our best DF result with uniform bins and the standard mixture path. Adjacent
bins also acquire similar output embeddings, suggesting that discrete heads can learn local geometry
without encoding it explicitly.

3
Discrete Action Prediction Can Match Continuous Parametrizations
In this section, we demonstrate that discrete action prediction matches, and often outperforms continuous
action prediction across both flow and regression policies. To evaluate performance, and refute possible
precision loss under action discretization, we measure (1) task success rate under closed-loop deployment,
and (2) mean ℓ2 action prediction error on held-out validation data (Appendix B.1).
Evaluation Protocol.
To demonstrate that our findings are broadly applicable, we consider a wide
breadth of tasks; we defer further implementation details to Appendix B.
• Single-Task Imitation Learning. To test whether discrete policies’ advantages depend on pretrained
VLM, we train separate policies from scratch, without a pretrained VLM backbone, on the Can, Square,
Tool Hang and Transport tasks in the RoboMimic suite [Mandlekar et al., 2021]. In particular Tool
Hang and Transport are representative of tasks that demand high control precision. Architectures and
training settings are in Appendix C.6.1.
• VLA Scale Tasks in sim. We post-train the PaliGemma Beyer et al. [2024] VLM, used by the π-series
models Black et al. [2025], Physical Intelligence et al. [2025b], with new action heads corresponding
to each action head parametrization. As representatives of VLA training, we train on one mixture of
eight sources: six RoboCasa365 datasets [Nasiriany et al., 2024, 2026], including MimicGen-generated
demonstrations [Mandlekar et al., 2023], LIBERO-plus [Fei et al., 2025], and DROID [Khazatsky
et al., 2024]. We measure closed-loop success on RoboCasa and LIBERO [Liu et al., 2023]; DROID
has no simulator and serves the offline diagnostics. Evaluations on RoboCasa are grouped into 3
categories: atomic-seen and composite-seen consisting of atomic and composite tasks, respectively, that
are contained in the training data; and composite-unseen, consisting of tasks that require compositions
of in-distribution atomic-seen, but in novel combinations. The training mixture is in Appendix B.4, the
architecture in Appendix C.6.2, and the evaluation protocol in Appendix B.3.

hypothesized, that discrete predictions are more compatible with token-based VLM backbones [Brohan

by precision, we present their end-effector reconstruction error in Fig. 2, showing that discrete policies
remain competitive (and in some cases slightly outperforms) their continuous counterparts. In fact, in
Fig. 2, we show that discrete flow policies in particular enjoy lower end-effector error early on in the
action chunk generation and along the finer motion regime, suggesting the potential for greater precision
when actions are discrete.
3.1
What Enables the Performance of Discrete Policies?
Paradoxically, our previous findings suggest that the opposite of the common wisdom regarding discrete
policies might be true: perhaps, discrete policies are uniformly better at high-precision action prediction
than their continuous counterparts?
However, this belief is not quite correct. Tab. 11 in Appendix F.2 shows that the ℓ2 reconstruction
errors across all parametrizations are roughly similar, and show no consistent correlation with task success.
For example, DR exhibits less error than CR on some benchmarks, and more on others, despite always
enjoying greater closed loop task success.

counterparts across the evaluated testbeds. They obtain stronger performance in both VLA and from-
scratch settings. These gains arise from better capturing variation across within-chunk time steps
and conditioning observations, rather than outright reconstruction error.
4
Factorization Error and Iterative Computation in Discrete Policies
Thus far, Section 3 establishes that discrete regression (DR) outperforms continuous regression (CR),
in part due to better capturing temporal variation, subtle action changes, and response to the input
variables. In this section, we show that the greater temporal reactivity observed in DR comes at the cost
of action inconsistency, or what we call factorization error (Finding A), harming policy performance. We
then identify that the key interventions which enhance performance—applying classifier-free-guidance
(Finding B) and introducing iterative computation by moving to discrete flow (Finding C) —correct this
inconsistency via complementary mechanisms described below.
Factorization Error in Discrete Policies.
DR predicts a categorical distribution for each action token,
giving the factorized distribution pθ(z | o) =
Q
j pθ,j(zj | o). When an observation admits several
plausible behaviors, different token distributions can assign probability to alternatives behaviors indepen-
dently. These independent choices can combine incompatible alternatives across time steps and action
dimensions. We refer to this failure to preserve dependencies between token choices as factorization
error. This may occur even when every token marginal is learned correctly, because the product of the
marginals need not recover the joint distribution of demonstrated chunks.
Evaluation Protocol for Factorization Error.
To isolate token ambiguity as a potential source of
incompatible action tokens, we vary (i) the number of function evaluations (NFE), that is, the number of
refinement steps N in DF, comparing to DR as a baseline, and (ii) the guidance weight λ of classifier-free
guidance (CFG, Section 2). Note that greater NFE provides more opportunity for self-correction, whereas
greater CFG “sharpens” the generated action distribution. Due to space constraints, policy performance
under varying CFG/NFE is reported in Appendix F.3; in short, performance improves with NFE and an
appropriate guidance weight.

Discussion and Future Work
We began by asking whether robot actions are fundamentally different from other AI modalities in
requiring continuous prediction. Our results challenge this distinction. Although robots operate in a
continuous physical world, simple action tokens can support precise control in both simulation and
real-world tasks. Their success shows that discrete action prediction is more than a convenient interface
to a language model. It is an effective way to learn robot control in its own right.
The key difficulty exposed by our experiments is deciding which token choices belong together.
Discrete heads readily express action variation, but independent predictions can combine incompatible
behaviors. Iterative refinement and guidance help resolve these conflicts while retaining responsiveness.
This connects action prediction to a challenge also studied in diffusion language models: accurate token
predictions must be coordinated to form a coherent joint output.
The need for joint consistency also suggests a different objective for action tokenization. Although
the tested reconstruction-based tokenizers do not improve on our best uniform-bin DF result, tokenizers
designed to make coupled action choices easier to predict may help reduce factorization error. For
example, tokens could jointly encode coherent motion across several time steps. Their value may depend
on the prediction head and the number of generation steps available. Finding tokenizations suited to
discrete regression, iterative flow, or generation with only a few refinement steps is therefore a promising
direction. Such tokenizers should be evaluated by the consistency and control performance of the
resulting policies, alongside reconstruction quality.
More broadly, these findings support learning action generation within the same token-based frame-
work as perception and reasoning. A natural next step is to test whether sharing representations and
prediction machinery across modalities improves co-training and transfer, while preserving the action
consistency needed for closed-loop control.These results make a strong case for treating discrete action
prediction as a central component of future robot foundation models.

A
Related Work
Action representations.
Early generalist policies predicted actions as tokens. RT-1 discretizes each
action coordinate into 256 uniform bins [Brohan et al., 2022], RT-2 and OpenVLA reuse such bins as
tokens of a pretrained vision-language model [Brohan et al., 2023, Kim et al., 2024], and Gato trains
one transformer from scratch on text and on actions in 1024 bins [Reed et al., 2022]. The field has
since moved to continuous heads, motivated by the precision of continuous outputs and by the ability
of diffusion models to represent multimodal action distributions. Diffusion Policy and ACT generate
continuous chunks [Chi et al., 2023, Zhao et al., 2023], Octo and RDT-1B use a diffusion head or
backbone [Octo Model Team et al., 2024, Liu et al., 2025], and most recent VLAs attach a diffusion or
flow-matching module to a pretrained VLM [Li et al., 2024a, Black et al., 2025, Physical Intelligence
et al., 2025b, NVIDIA et al., 2025, Shukor et al., 2025] or regress actions directly [Kim et al., 2025].
Methods that keep discrete actions usually add machinery to address a perceived weakness of plain bins.
Behavior Transformers add a continuous offset to a discrete cluster to recover precision [Shafiullah et al.,
2022, Cui et al., 2023, Lee et al., 2024]. FAST, vector and scalar quantization, and B-spline tokenizers
compress chunks into shorter token sequences [Pertsch et al., 2025, Lee et al., 2024, Belkhale and Sadigh,
2024, Wang et al., 2025b, Mete et al., 2024, Zhou et al., 2025]. HybridVLA and knowledge insulation
combine a discrete token loss with a continuous action objective in one model [Liu et al., 2026a, Driess
et al., 2025]. We ask whether this machinery is needed. Our discrete heads predict plain per-coordinate
uniform bins, and Section 5 tests several of these tokenizers.
Comparisons of discrete and continuous heads.
The move to continuous heads rests partly on studies
that swap the action head within one model and find continuous heads stronger [Octo Model Team
et al., 2024, Li et al., 2024b, Kim et al., 2025, Hou et al., 2025, Wu et al., 2026b, Ye et al., 2026a].
Octo, RoboVLMs, and OpenVLA-OFT attribute the gap to bin precision. But these studies change the
representation together with other choices. The discrete head usually decodes tokens autoregressively,
and FAST shows that autoregressive decoding of per-step bins fails at 20 Hz and 50 Hz [Pertsch et al.,
2025]. The discrete head is often a linear projection onto the language-model vocabulary while the
continuous head is a new MLP [Li et al., 2024b, Kim et al., 2025], and the discrete baselines in Diffusion
Policy and ACT predict one action at a time [Chi et al., 2023, Zhao et al., 2023]. When all tokens are
decoded in one pass, the gap shrinks or reverses. RT-1’s one-pass bins outperform a unimodal Gaussian
head [Brohan et al., 2022], BEAST’s parallel tokens outperform L1 regression on the same B-spline
representation [Zhou et al., 2025], BAKU’s BeT and VQ-BeT heads match an MLP head and match or
beat a diffusion head [Haldar et al., 2024], and Discrete Diffusion VLA reports 94.2% LIBERO success
for parallel bins, 81.2% for autoregressive bins, and 94.9% for a continuous diffusion head [Liang et al.,
2026b]. The evidence is still not one-sided: parallel bins trail L1 regression in OpenVLA-OFT (90.2% vs.
95.3%) [Kim et al., 2025], and the ranking of heads in Dream-VLA changes with the backbone [Ye et al.,
2025a]. Separating the representation from the decoding requires varying them independently. Pan et al.
[2026] do this for continuous heads, comparing regression and flow under matched architectures. We
extend the comparison to discrete heads, crossing continuous and discrete representations with one-pass
and iterative generation under one backbone and training recipe.
Discrete diffusion and flow policies.
A second line of work keeps discrete actions and replaces
autoregressive decoding with iterative parallel decoding. Discrete Diffusion VLA decodes 256-bin action
tokens inside the VLM by confidence-ordered unmasking [Liang et al., 2026b]. Some VLAs build on
diffusion language models: LLaDA-VLA and MMaDA-VLA start from one [Wen et al., 2025b, Liu et al.,
2026b], and Dream-VLA pretrains one on discrete action tokens before fine-tuning a flow head [Ye et al.,
2025a]. A shared token space also lets one model generate actions together with text and images. dVLA,

UD-VLA, and MMaDA-VLA denoise these tokens jointly [Wen et al., 2025a, Chen et al., 2026a, Liu et al.,
2026b], and MM-ACT co-trains the three generation tasks [Liang et al., 2026a]. Later work targets speed
and deployment through block diffusion and caching [Lee et al., 2026, Wang et al., 2026b, Song et al.,

and RL fine-tuning [Wu et al., 2026c]. The approach also extends to policies trained without a VLM [He
et al., 2024, Zhuang et al., 2026] and to driving [Li et al., 2026c, Xu et al., 2025, Dang et al., 2026].
Closest to our DF head, DFM-VLA and WAM-Flow use discrete flow matching with a uniform source
and a metric-induced path [Chen et al., 2026b, Xu et al., 2026], and E0 trains a π0-style expert with
cross-entropy on Gaussian-noised one-hot bins [Zhan et al., 2025]. This work focuses on building faster
and more capable models, and leaves open why iterative decoding helps. Several of these papers find
that it beats one-pass decoding in the same model [Liang et al., 2026b, Lee et al., 2026, Liang et al.,
2026a], but fewer than half train a continuous head on the same backbone, none of the manipulation
policies applies classifier-free guidance to action tokens, and DFM-VLA and WAM-Flow do not compare
the metric path to a uniform-source mixture path with all else fixed. Section 4 attributes the gain of
iterative decoding to reduced factorization error and adds guidance, and Section 5 tests metric-induced
paths.
Discrete diffusion and flow models.
Our DF head builds on discrete diffusion [Austin et al., 2021,
Hoogeboom et al., 2021, Campbell et al., 2022], in particular its masked variant [Lou et al., 2024,
Sahoo et al., 2024, Shi et al., 2024, Ou et al., 2025, Chang et al., 2022, 2023], which now scales to
large language models [Nie et al., 2025, Ye et al., 2025b], and on discrete flow matching [Gat et al.,
2024, Campbell et al., 2024]. Shaul et al. [2025] extend discrete flow matching to arbitrary paths and
propose the metric-induced path tested in Section 5. Uniform sources and remasking samplers, which
let committed tokens change, are related alternatives [Schiff et al., 2025, Sahoo et al., 2025, Deng
et al., 2026, Wang et al., 2025a, Zhao et al., 2025]. Most relevant to us, few-step sampling in these
models suffers the same factorization error as one-pass action heads, because each step predicts positions
independently. ReDi and CoDD reduce this error [Yoo et al., 2025, Li et al., 2026a], and Tang et al.
[2026] show that it persists even with an exact denoiser. Section 4 studies the same error for action
chunks.
Discretized control and classification losses.
Section 3 shows that discrete heads can match con-
tinuous ones despite quantization. Work outside imitation learning suggests why precision is rarely
the bottleneck. In reinforcement learning, per-dimension action bins work well on continuous-control
benchmarks [Metz et al., 2017, Tavakoli et al., 2018, Tang and Agrawal, 2020, Seyde et al., 2021, 2023,
Chebotar et al., 2023], Dactyl uses only 11 bins per coordinate [OpenAI et al., 2020], and value and
reward heads are often trained with cross-entropy over bins [Bellemare et al., 2017, Hafner et al., 2025,
Hansen et al., 2024]. HL-Gauss, a cross-entropy loss with Gaussian-smoothed bin targets [Imani and
White, 2018], improves the performance and scaling of deep RL [Farebrother et al., 2024]. Analyses
attribute such gains to training rather than to modelling the target distribution: easier optimization in
supervised regression [Imani et al., 2026], robustness to noisy targets and better representations in RL
[Farebrother et al., 2024], and different learned features in two-layer networks [Stewart et al., 2023].
MSE also has a known failure mode. It averages distinct modes [Shafiullah et al., 2022] and smooths
over discontinuities [Florence et al., 2021], and in forecasting, models trained with MSE or quantile
losses regress toward the mean or median when the context is uncertain, while cross-entropy over bins
can settle on a mode [Yu et al., 2026]. For behavior cloning, the regret with quantized actions and a
log-loss depends on the quantization error only polynomially in the horizon, given stable dynamics and
a probabilistically smooth policy [Cao and Xie, 2026]. Categorical outputs are also standard for other
continuous signals such as pixels and audio [van den Oord et al., 2016b,a]. For VLAs the evidence is

Classifier-free guidance.
Section 4 uses classifier-free guidance [Ho and Salimans, 2022] to sharpen
discrete action distributions. For categorical outputs, guidance reweights each token’s distribution
directly. Muse applies it to token logits [Chang et al., 2023], later work formalizes it for discrete diffusion

it lowers entropy while also reordering the top tokens [Sanchez et al., 2024]. We apply the Muse rule
to each coordinate’s clean-token distribution. In continuous diffusion the effect is less direct. Guided

and gives a small gain for language-conditioned flow VLAs [Park et al., 2026], but it is slightly worse

B.1
Evaluation Measures and Comparison Protocol
Settings.
Our evidence spans pretrained vision–language–action policies, evaluated on RoboCasa
and LIBERO and diagnosed offline on DROID, and policies trained from scratch on RoboMimic and
Push-T. Controlled function fitting uses separate models with a common trunk architecture to predict
deterministic targets from a scalar input. These settings let us examine precision in robot policies and
study fitting behavior where the target and codec error are known.
Evaluating action quality.
We distinguish three measurements. Prediction error compares predicted
and demonstrated actions, reporting training fit and held-out error separately. Robot translation errors
use millimeters; Push-T uses pixels. Temporal variation, assessed through jerk, describes variation in
the commanded trajectory. Task performance measures closed-loop success on RoboCasa, LIBERO, and
RoboMimic in Figure 1.
Robot evaluations use 5 evaluation seeds with per-head checkpoints and inference budgets. Synthetic
comparisons use 3 initialization seeds. Formal error definitions, benchmark recipes, and the chronology
of the synthetic studies are in Appendices B and H.

B.3
VLA-Scale Experiments
All VLA-scale policies are trained on one shared data mixture (Appendix B.4). They share one PaliGemma
backbone, action horizon 24, quantile normalization statistics, and seed 7. Actions are relative end-
effector commands for a Franka arm: target poses with respect to the end-effector pose at prediction
time, with axis-angle rotation. For each head, we evaluate the last five saved checkpoints, at 110k, 120k,
130k, 140k, and 150k training steps, and report the best of the five. Each evaluation uses five seeds; we
report the mean success rate over the seeds, and ± denotes the standard deviation over them. Evaluation
replans every 24 steps, and every head runs the same evaluation episodes. The backbone, optimizer, and
batch size are given in Appendix C.6.2.
RoboCasa.
RoboCasa [Nasiriany et al., 2026] is a simulated benchmark of household manipulation tasks
in kitchen scenes. We run 10 trials per task on three suites: atomic-seen (180 episodes), composite-seen
(160), and composite-unseen (160).
LIBERO.
LIBERO [Liu et al., 2023] is a simulated benchmark of language-conditioned tabletop manip-
ulation with a Franka arm. Each suite has 10 tasks with 50 human demonstrations per task. We evaluate
closed-loop success on two suites. In LIBERO-Object, the tasks share one layout and differ in which object
the robot picks and places in a basket. LIBERO-10 (also called LIBERO-Long) has long-horizon tasks
that each chain two subtasks across varied objects, layouts, and scenes. The training mixture contains
LIBERO-plus demonstrations (Appendix B.4), and held-out LIBERO-plus episodes are a validation source
for the offline diagnostics.
DROID.
DROID [Khazatsky et al., 2024] is a real-world Franka teleoperation dataset of 76k trajectories
collected across 564 scenes and 86 tasks. It is part of the VLA training mixture. DROID has no simulator,
so we do not measure closed-loop success on it. Held-out DROID chunks instead serve as a validation
source for the offline diagnostics: the jerk ratio and EEF error in Fig. 2, the t-SNE in Fig. 4(d), and the
CFG error sweep in Appendix E.2.
B.4
VLA Training Data Mixture
The VLA-scale policies train on one mixture of eight sources: six RoboCasa365 datasets [Nasiriany et al.,
2026], LIBERO-plus [Fei et al., 2025], and DROID [Khazatsky et al., 2024] (Tab. 2). All eight share
one action space, a 7-dimensional relative end-effector command for a Franka arm: 3D position and
axis-angle rotation with respect to the end-effector pose at prediction time, and gripper. One set of
quantile normalization statistics covers the whole mixture.
We filter frames before sampling. For RoboCasa, we drop the frames in which the robot drives its
mobile base and keep the arm-control frames. For DROID, we keep the successful episodes and remove
idle segments. LIBERO-plus is used in full. Each source is sampled with weight proportional to the square
root of its frame count. The RoboCasa MimicGen source has 116M frames of synthetic demonstrations.
To keep it from dominating the mixture, its frame count is capped at DROID’s when we compute the
weight. We hold out 2% of the episodes of every source, chosen with a fixed seed. These held-out
episodes form the eight validation sources for the offline diagnostics.

Discrete-Flow Source Distribution and Sampler
The source factorizes as q0(z0) =
QL
j=1 q0,j(z0,j). A uniform source has q0,j(k) = 1/B for k ∈[B] [Gat
et al., 2024]; a masked source places all mass on an auxiliary mask token [Sahoo et al., 2024]. The
mask is an input symbol and does not decode to an action bin. In either case, the clean-token predictor
outputs distributions over [B].
The schedule is continuously differentiable and nondecreasing, with α(0) = 0, α(1) = 1, and
α(t) < 1 for t < 1. For the grid 0 = t0 < ··· < tN = 1, the mixture-path update in Equation (2.3)
uses ηn = (α(tn+1) −α(tn))/(1 −α(tn)). Proposals and replacement decisions are independent across
positions conditional on the current chunk. This rule gives an Euler approximation, in the interpolation
coordinate α, to the associated continuous-time Markov chain. Because ηN−1 = 1, the final update
replaces every position with a clean-token proposal, so the output contains no mask tokens.
The policy is πDF
θ,N(o;ξ) := DQ(ztN ), where ξ collects randomness in initialization, proposals, and re-
placement decisions. We suppress N and ξ when clear from context. For a uniform source, tokens updated
at earlier steps may be revised later. Subsequent predictions condition on the updated chunk, allowing
the generation process to capture dependencies across positions despite the conditional independence of
updates within each step.
C.5
Classifier-Free Guidance
CFG [Ho and Salimans, 2022] combines a prediction conditioned on the observation o with one made
from a null condition ;.
Condition and null condition.
Let c be the encoding of the observation o. For the VLA policies, c is the
set of 64 condition tokens that the resampler produces from the backbone states and the proprioception
token (Appendix C.6.2), so it carries the images, the instruction, and the proprioceptive state. The null
condition ; is a learned set of 64 tokens with the same shape. During training, each example’s condition
tokens are replaced by ; with probability 0.1, so the same head learns the conditional prediction and an
unconditional prediction that sees no observation. All other head inputs (the current chunk, the flow
time, and the auxiliary random inputs) are unchanged. We write ; in place of o for predictions made
with the null condition, as in pθ,j(k | ;), vθ(xt, t,;), and πCR
θ (;).

Why CR is not guided.
Guidance reweights a distribution. For DR and DF it tilts each token’s
distribution, and for CF it changes the velocity field that carries noise to actions. CR has no distribution
to reweight: it outputs one chunk, its estimate of E[a1:H | o], and its unconditional output estimates

the conditional mean already averages them, and moving it does not select one of them. We therefore
evaluate CR without guidance.
Inference cost.
The backbone and resampler encode o once per inference call. Because ; is a fixed
learned tensor, the unconditional prediction needs no backbone pass, only a second pass of the action
head. We report NFE as the number of steps N for DF and CF. Guidance doubles the action-head passes,
from one to two for DR and from N to 2N for DF and CF, and leaves the backbone cost unchanged.
Under this convention, λ = 0 gives the unguided conditional prediction.

C.6.1
Single-Task From-Scratch Architectures
RoboMimic policies observe the object state and the end-effector position, orientation, and gripper state
of each arm, over the last two time steps. An MLP encodes the flattened observation. Each policy predicts
H = 10 actions and executes 8 before replanning. An action is a 10-dimensional relative end-effector
command: 3D position, 6D rotation, and gripper (20 dimensions for the two-arm Transport task). Actions
are normalized per dimension to [−1,1]. Discrete heads use B = 512 bins per coordinate. Tab. 4 lists
the two network families.
CF integrates its velocity field with 9 Euler steps. DF runs 100 update steps with the mixture path
κt = t3, followed by one argmax prediction. All models are trained for 300k steps with AdamW (learning
rate 10−4, weight decay 10−5, gradient clipping at 10) and an exponential moving average of the weights
with decay 0.995, which is used for evaluation. Continuous heads use batch size 1024 and cosine decay;
discrete heads use batch size 512, a 5% linear warmup, and cosine decay. Each run uses one L40S GPU.
The synthetic-fitting trunk is described in Appendix H.

E.3
State Responsiveness and Disturbance Stability
Question Can a policy stay stable under small disturbances while still responding to real state
changes?
We measure two properties of how a head’s predictions change with the observation. State re-
sponsiveness asks whether the predicted chunk changes as much as the demonstrated chunk when the
state changes along a real trajectory. Disturbance stability asks how little the distribution of predicted
chunks moves after a small disturbance, compared with the head’s own sampling variation. The two pull
against each other: a head that changes little with its input is stable but under-responsive. Flow heads
have the highest disturbance stability, and within the flow family DF is more responsive than CF. The
state-responsiveness result in Fig. 3 is the lag-4 row of Tab. 10.

F.1
Experimental Methodology
Mode-detection criteria.
For each translation-token distribution pj(b), we smooth probabilities along
the bin axis with a Gaussian kernel of standard deviation two bins, normalized over the available bins
at each location. We identify local maxima, including endpoint maxima, in the smoothed distribution.
Each bin is assigned to its nearest peak, and each peak’s mass is the sum of the unsmoothed probabilities
assigned to it. We retain peaks with at least 10% mass. Two adjacent retained peaks qualify as separated
modes if they are at least eight bins apart and the minimum smoothed probability between them is at
most 50% of the smaller peak height. Each translation token represents one coordinate at one predicted
time step. We call it ambiguous if it has a qualifying pair of modes, and count it once regardless of how
many such pairs it has. The mean count averages ambiguous tokens per chunk. The threshold statistic
reports the fraction of chunks containing at least k ambiguous tokens. For DF, detection is performed on
each final conditional token prediction before averaging over refinement draws.

best pooled success rates are 52.8% and 4.6%, respectively. The bin-progress clock with p = 1 on the
equal-frequency representation reaches 51.8%. Each rate uses one operating point across all three suites.
The first result is competitive with, but does not exceed, the mixture-path DF result of 53.9%. The effect
of explicit geometry depends strongly on how the path is constructed.

The robot experiments raise a simple question: can a model get the broad motion right and still miss the
fine detail? Robot data mixes too many factors to answer this, so we use synthetic targets where we
control the fine structure directly.

and so has no finite Lipschitz constant. CF and DF both predict clean targets.

Controls narrow the explanation.
Quantized Regression fits the bin centers with squared error. It
behaves like CR on both targets, so discrete target values are not what drives DR’s result. CF tracks CR

One likely reason is argmax itself. The logits can change smoothly while the decoded bin jumps, which
